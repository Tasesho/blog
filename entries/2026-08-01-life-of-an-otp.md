Hewwo people!

The title may be confusing because of the term OTP. In this essay, I'm referring to One Trick Pony. I first came across this term when I started playing League of Legends back in 2020. At the time, I only played Master Yi—for about six months, I played almost nothing but Master Yi. This "fear the man who has practiced the same move 1,000 times" mentality has affected my life in both good and bad ways.

In this essay, I'll be sharing some personal thoughts and experiences, along with the advantages and disadvantages of this mentality.

# OTP

As mentioned before, OTP stands for One Trick Pony. It means you've trained so much in one specific thing that you've become highly skilled at it, but you may struggle with everything else.

This term is mostly used in video games, where you can master a single character, build, weapon, class, or game mode.

However, I think we can also apply this idea to everyday life. In my case, it can be something as simple as always choosing the same ice cream flavor (chocolate mint, in my case) for years.

We can even apply this to academics. Whenever I'm genuinely interested in a topic, I tend to research it deeply. This has happened to me with subjects such as Object-Oriented Programming, Linux, and System Administration.

I genuinely enjoy focusing on one thing and becoming the best I can at it.

## What pros I've found

Just to be clear, you can't become an OTP overnight. There's a big difference between doing deep research on something and becoming obsessed with it.

One of the biggest advantages of being an OTP is developing a completely different understanding of what you're doing. For example, when I was a Master Yi OTP, I discovered several useful off-meta builds that were surprisingly fun to play. I also learned that Master Yi isn't really a scaling champion—he's a snowball champion. This kind of knowledge helped me think differently from the average Yi player.

## What cons does it have?

The most obvious downside is that you become very good at the one thing you chose, but you may be completely unprepared for everything else. And I want to be clear about this: I don't mean that you have no value as a person. I mean that you may not have the knowledge required to perform a different task within the same field.

Back then, I was the 32nd-best Master Yi player in the world. But if I couldn't play Yi, my skills weren't much better than those of a Bronze player.

The same thing happened to me in programming. I knew how to design classes in C++ with useful methods, but I struggled with Data Structures because I had spent so much time studying Object-Oriented Programming instead.

## Refusing to Be a Beginner

In my opinion, no one wants to be a beginner again, but sometimes it's inevitable, especially in the IT industry. New technologies appear all the time, and even older ones are worth revisiting.

In my experience, learning about something I'm not genuinely interested in is tedious. I simply don't enjoy it. Staying in your comfort zone feels safe, and once you're there, it's difficult to let go.

There's also a psychological aspect to this. You may have some form of neurodivergence without even being aware of it. I'm not saying that's the reason in every case, but I do think it can influence the way some people become deeply attached to a single interest.

## Outside Games

As I mentioned before, being an OTP is almost like a philosophy to me. It can be something as simple as always choosing the same ice cream flavor, or as complex as diving into the same technology rabbit hole for years.

Personally, I don't see this as a disadvantage because I've lived this way for so long that it feels completely natural. However, I also recognize there are areas where I could probably do better if I weren't so attached to doing the same things.

Let's start with some simple examples.

1. **My comfort snack.** This one is a bit tricky because everyone probably has a favorite snack they always come back to. Mine is Peanut ChocoRolls with an energy drink. I absolutely love that combination, and this year marks four years of buying exactly the same thing whenever I'm in the mood for a snack.

2. **The same sneakers.** I've been buying the exact same pair of shoes since 2024: the DC Shoes Court Graffik. They're incredibly comfortable, durable, and I don't even have to tie the laces.

Now for something more meaningful.

3. **The same career path.** I first became interested in IT around 2017 while researching Arduino development. As you may know, Arduino uses a C++-based language. I enjoyed programming so much that I eventually moved on to C++ itself, and that language has stayed with me ever since.

Even today, whenever I start learning something new, I usually find myself asking how I can connect it back to C++. Whether it's game development, embedded systems, or even operating systems, C++ somehow finds its way back into the picture.

## Wrapping Up

The purpose of this essay isn't to convince you to become an OTP. Instead, I wanted to show that this mentality can exist far beyond video games.

For me, repeating the same thing over and over isn't necessarily a problem. In fact, I believe that repetition allows me to understand something deeply enough to discover approaches that most people would never think about.

However, I also don't want to paint this mindset as something perfect.

More than once, this mentality has worked against me. It has made me struggle with adapting to different situations, whether that meant having a poor champion pool in a game or burning myself out from endlessly researching and studying the same subject.

I think this is something that depends on each person. Some people are naturally more flexible than I am, while others may become even more attached to their favorite interests.

## Final Reflections

Being an OTP has shaped the way I learn, the way I play games, and even the way I make everyday decisions.

I don't regret it. It taught me discipline, persistence, and the value of understanding something beyond the surface level. Many of the skills I have today came from spending hundreds of hours focused on a single topic.

At the same time, I've learned that there is a difference between mastering something and refusing to grow beyond it.

Nowadays, I still enjoy going down rabbit holes and becoming obsessed with a subject. The difference is that I'm trying to become comfortable with being a beginner again.

After all, every expert was once a beginner.

And maybe the hardest part isn't learning something new.

Maybe it's allowing yourself to start from zero once again.
